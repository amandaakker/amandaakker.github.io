Case study 001 · Mobile app

Bubble
Breaker

One local question a day, answered in a tap, to show you what your neighbors really think.

Role

Sole designer: research, ideation, UI, testing

Timeline

~5 months, UW UX certificate program

Research

6 user interviews, 12 usability tests

Tools

Figma

The problem

How might we help people understand perspectives different from their own, and build more supportive, equitable relationships?

Most of us spend our days around people who already agree with us. Social media amplifies it. I wanted to find a low-pressure way for people to see how their own community thinks, without asking them to jump into a heated comment thread.

Research

How people meet other viewpoints today

I ran exploratory interviews with six people, recruited through friends of friends, all regular social media users. I wanted to understand how they consume news, how they behave online, and where they run into opinions unlike their own.

What is your current experience interacting with people who have different perspectives from yourself? What are the biggest challenges in meeting people outside of your normal circle? How do you use media to learn about current events?

What I heard:

  • Most participants mainly interacted with people who shared their views.
  • They valued seeing other people's opinions, even when they didn't join the discussion.
  • Everyday life offered few chances to engage with different perspectives.
Personas
Persona A

The Casual User

"I like to hear what other people have to say, even though I might not agree with it. I don't really interact. I'm more like a viewer."
  • Browses social media but rarely comments
  • Drawn in by pictures and visuals
  • Motivated by interesting topics and others' opinions
Persona B

The Researcher

"Technology is designed to isolate. It takes more action and self-awareness to break out of one's usual social circles."
  • Joins discussions and digs deeper on topics
  • Encouraged by differing opinions
  • Motivated by truly understanding an issue

They engage very differently, but both enjoy browsing interesting topics and want to learn new perspectives. The design had to serve the quiet viewer and the active debater at once.

Key insights
01

Low-lift participation

Taking part can't require real time. People should contribute something in seconds and see results right away.

02

Show what's outside the bubble

The world has plenty of social apps. This one has to surface viewpoints beyond your circle and make them easy to explore.

03

Simplicity builds trust

Keep attention on the community's perspectives. A clear, uncluttered interface lowers cognitive load on a sensitive topic.

The pivot

Research changed the product

Original idea

A general social network connecting people across cities and industries.

→
After research

A light daily poll on local current events. Answer anonymously in one tap; comment only if you want to.

Interviews made it clear that people didn't want another feed to keep up with. A single daily question respects their time, works for passive viewers and active debaters alike, and gives a reason to come back tomorrow.

How it works
  1. Get one community poll each day, tied to a local headline
  2. Answer anonymously in seconds
  3. See how your city answered, broken down by group
  4. Read comments and perspectives from neighbors
  5. Revisit past topics in the archive
User journey
Low fidelity
Iterations

Onboarding

What I heard

Too many required fields at sign-up felt overwhelming.

What I changed

Cut the required fields down to the essentials.

Result

Testers found sign-up much easier, with less to fill in up front.

Question of the day

What I heard

A topic summary without the question itself was confusing, and people bounced between screens.

What I changed

Reduced the text, put the question up front, and used headlines and photos for context.

Result

Testers understood the question immediately and navigated with less effort.

Final designs

The results screen does the most work: it shows how the city answered, then where opinions diverge by group, which is the moment users said they found most interesting.

Impact
12/12

participants completed the core task: submitting a poll response and viewing community results.

92%

said they were interested in using the app regularly. Comparing opinions with locals was the biggest draw.

"I love statistics and seeing what other people in my area actually think. I think this would drive meaningful conversations with my friends."Usability test participant
"The comment feature and ability to see opinions from strangers in my area seems promising to meet more people with opposing views."Usability test participant
Reflection

What I learned

  • User feedback matters at every stage, not just the end.
  • Assumptions about usability should always be validated through testing.
  • Small, continuous iterations add up to a much more intuitive product.

Next, I'd expand the results dashboard with deeper breakdowns, tighten visual details like reaction icons, add reminders and a personal voting history, and let people compare results across cities.

Case study 002 · Web platform · In progress

Tastely

Limited-time restaurant drops, in one place, before they sell out.

Role

Sole designer: research, ideation, UI, testing

Timeline

March 2026 – present

Research

8 interviews and usability tests

Tools

Figma

The problem

How might diners find and order limited-time menu items that restaurants rarely advertise?

Seasonal specials and secret-menu dishes are some of the most exciting things a restaurant makes, but they usually live on a chalkboard or an Instagram story. If you don't already follow the restaurant, you miss them.

Research

Why people try something new

I interviewed eight people, recruited through friends of friends, about how they choose where to eat and what makes them order something unfamiliar.

When eating out, how do you decide what restaurants to go to? What draws you to trying something new vs. sticking to familiar options? How do you feel about mystery items in general, whether it's food or a different product?

What I heard:

  • Even people who don't call themselves foodies enjoy trying new places nearby.
  • Visually distinctive, "Instagrammable" dishes strongly drive interest.
  • Limited-time and seasonal items create real urgency and excitement.
  • Surprise is appealing, as long as it feels low-risk and fun.
Personas
Persona A

The Foodie

"I want to try any food and any restaurant. If there's a new option, especially if it's unique or Instagrammable, I'll buy it."
  • Actively seeks new restaurants and trends
  • Loves visually distinctive experiences
  • Motivated by discovery and sharing
Persona B

The Experimenter

"I love to try seasonal or limited edition items. I want something I can't make at home or buy from a grocery store."
  • Open to new restaurants, but selective
  • Looks for exclusivity and uniqueness
  • Motivated by quality and novelty

Both want dining experiences they can't easily get at home. They differ in how much exploring they're willing to do.

Key insights
01

Discovery drives engagement

People are motivated by finding something new. Even non-foodies lean in when an experience feels special.

02

Exclusivity creates urgency

People are more likely to visit when they might miss out. Limited quantities turn interest into action.

03

Novelty needs low risk

Users want adventure without committing to a total unknown. Clear photos and details make trying something new feel safe.

The pivot

From "new restaurants" to "new dishes"

Original idea

A platform for discovering restaurants in your area you haven't been to yet.

→
After research

A feed of limited-time "drops" from local restaurants, organized around the dish itself.

Participants told me there's no shortage of restaurant options. What they lacked was a reason to pick one. Exclusive, time-limited dishes narrow the choice and add urgency, which helps diners and gives restaurants a way to reach new and returning customers.

How it works
  1. Local restaurants post exclusive or limited-time menu items
  2. Diners browse today's drops near them, with remaining quantities shown
  3. Reserve or order an item before it runs out
  4. Pick up, try it, and leave a quick rating for the restaurant
User journey
Low fidelity
Iterations

Organizing the drops

What I heard

Grouping by restaurant added confusion, since most restaurants only have one or two drops at a time.

What I changed

Organized everything around the dish itself, led by a large photo.

Result

The food photo up front immediately caught testers' attention.

Post-order feedback

What I heard

Too many required questions. New users wouldn't finish the survey.

What I changed

Kept only the questions most useful to restaurants, and made few of them required.

Result

Testers were more likely to complete the whole survey and leave a comment.

Final designs
Impact
8/8

participants completed every core task without help: browsing drops, adding to cart, and checking out.

8/8

said they were interested in using the platform, citing discovery and seasonal items as key motivators.

"This would be super cool to see what limited time menu items there are. Especially when there's seasonal items, and you might not have the chance to try it again."Usability test participant
"This makes it easy to choose directly instead of just scrolling through endless options. It makes me excited to go to the restaurant, and share my experience with friends."Usability test participant
Reflection

What I learned

Narrowing the focus made the product more distinctive. Plenty of apps help people find new restaurants, but very few focus on limited-time dishes.

Next, I'd add filters by food type or vibe, personalized recommendations based on past orders and ratings, test a rewards system for repeat visits, and partner with real restaurants to validate the business side.
